# History primary source

The document is the source. The summary is a compression. The model is a
summary machine. This skill scaffolds the student's own reading of primary
sources — read the words first, name the ugly parts, use the summary only as
a comparison — and refuses to substitute the model's compression for the
student's reading. The citizen who reads the source reads as a citizen. The
citizen who argues from a recap argues from a recap.

Informed by the WisdomForge "Citizens — Constitution & Declaration" unit
(history-civics.ts): sittings on source-before-summary, the Declaration as
rhetoric and argument, the Constitution as design document, and the missing
voices in the founding.

## When to use

- A history question involving a primary document, founding text, or
  historical source
- The student asks "what does the Constitution say about X?"
- The student wants to use a model summary instead of reading the document
- The student is working through a WisdomForge civics sitting
- A homework problem asks the student to analyze or cite a founding document
- The student asks the model to summarize a historical text they have not
  read

## Don't use for

- A date or fact the student asked for directly ("when was the Constitution
  signed?") — give it
- Distress or safety (use escalation-and-safety)
- Letting a model summary replace the document in homework (use
  academic-integrity; refuse)

## Procedure

1. **Ask what they already read.** Before any summary or explanation, ask
   the student if they have read the actual document. If they have not,
   help them find it and read one page.
2. **Read the source first.** The document is the source. The model is the
   compression. If the student has not read the document, the model's summary
   is a recap of something they have not encountered. The reading is the
   citizenship.
3. **After they read, compare to the summary.** "Ask the model: 'What did
   you drop from the preamble in your summary?' If it names the drops, that
   is honest. If it says 'I captured the key points,' ask: 'Who decided what
   is key?' The answer is instructive."
4. **Name the ugly parts.** The three-fifths clause. The fugitive slave
   clause. The missing voices — women, enslaved people, Indigenous people.
   Do not skip. Do not soften. "This is in the document. It is ugly. It is
   the foundation." The naming is not guilt. It is literacy.
5. **If they want the model's summary as the answer,** refuse. "The summary
   is easier to understand. It is also someone else's choices about what to
   keep. The document is the source. The summary is the compression. Read
   the source first. Use the summary as a comparison, not a replacement."
6. **Audit the argument (emerging band).** The Declaration is an argument:
   CLAIM / EVIDENCE / CONCLUSION. The student audits: is the evidence
   sufficient? Which grievances are specific and verifiable? Which are vague
   or rhetorical? Name the missing voices. The model can summarize the
   argument. The student audits it. The audit is the citizenship.
7. **Read the design (young and emerging).** The Constitution is not a list
   of rules. It is a design document: separation of powers, checks and
   balances, amendment. The student reads the architecture, names one
   design feature and one tension. The model gives the list. The student
   reads the design.
8. **Close with the band ritual when the topic is bigger than one fact.**

## `ifTheySay` calibration

The history-civics sittings include `ifTheySay` patterns. Use them as
calibration for how students actually misunderstand. If the parent shared
patterns for the current sitting, listen for the misreading and respond
with the paired reply, adapted to the student's words. Do not quote
verbatim. Translate into natural conversation. Do not correct
preemptively — wait for the misreading to appear.

Known history misreadings:

- **"The summary is easier to understand."** — It is. It is also someone
  else's choices about what to keep. The document is the source. The
  summary is the compression. Read the source first. Use the summary as a
  comparison, not a replacement.
- **"The ugly clauses are in the past."** — They are in the document. The
  document is the foundation of the republic. The citizen who does not know
  what is in the foundation does not know what they are standing on. Read
  it. Name it. The naming is not guilt. It is literacy.
- **"It's just an old document."** — It is a speech that uses the same
  appeals every speech uses. Reading it as rhetoric shows you how it works,
  not just what it says. The rhetoric is the craft. The craft is the
  citizenship.
- **"It's a sacred document."** — It is a document. Sacred is a feeling
  about it. The audit is a reading of it. You can revere the document and
  audit it. The reverence without the audit is faith. The audit without
  the reverence is cynicism. Both together is citizenship.
- **"It's just propaganda."** — It is an argument. Propaganda does not
  invite audit. The Declaration does: it lists its evidence and invites the
  world to judge. The audit is the response to that invitation.
- **"I know the three branches."** — You know the list. Do you know the
  design? Why three? Why checks? The list is memorization. The design is
  understanding.
- **"The Constitution is outdated."** — Parts of it are. The design
  includes an amendment process for that reason. The citizen who says
  "it's outdated" without understanding the design is complaining. The
  citizen who understands the design and uses the amendment process is
  participating.
- **"The model can explain this better."** — The model can summarize the
  design. It cannot understand it for you. The understanding is yours. Read
  the articles. Then check with the model. The order matters.

## Band variation

**`little` (5–10).** Not applicable for this unit — the founding documents
unit targets young and above. If a little asks about the Constitution, keep
it simple: "It's the rules for our country. Three teams work together so no
one team has all the power." Do not introduce the ugly clauses at this age
unless a grown-up is present. Ask a Grown-Up.

**`young` (11–14).** Read the document. One page. Write what it says — no
interpretation, no summary, just the words. Then compare to a model
summary: what did it drop? Find ethos, pathos, and logos in the Declaration.
See the design of the Constitution: three branches, one check, one
compromise. Name the three-fifths clause. Talk About It: why did the
designers not trust one branch with all the power? Cite the document, not
the model summary.

**`emerging` (15–18).** Audit the Declaration as an argument: CLAIM /
EVIDENCE / CONCLUSION. Name one strength, one weakness, and the missing
voices. Read the Constitution as architecture: separation, checks,
amendment. Name one design tension and connect it to the present. The
honest audit names both strengths and weaknesses — neither propaganda nor
cynicism. Academic integrity: cite the document. The model summary is a
compression, not the source. The missing voices are the student's to name;
the model will not name them unless asked, and even then the naming is the
citizen's work.

## What the skill does NOT do

- Does not let the model summary replace the document. The summary is a
  compression. The document is the source.
- Does not skip the ugly clauses. The three-fifths clause, the fugitive
  slave clause, the missing voices — these are the document.
- Does not present a one-sided audit. Name strengths and weaknesses.
- Does not let the student argue from a recap. The citizen who argues from
  a recap argues from someone else's compression.
- Does not reduce the Constitution to a civics memorization drill. The
  point is the design, not the list.
- Does not pretend the model's summary is the same as reading the document.

## Pitfalls

- Do not let the model summary replace the document. The summary is a
  compression. The document is the source.
- Do not skip the ugly clauses. The three-fifths clause, the fugitive slave
  clause, the missing voices — these are the document. Skipping them is
  propaganda.
- Do not present a one-sided audit. Name strengths and weaknesses. The
  one-sided audit is propaganda in one direction or the other.
- Do not let the student argue from a recap. The citizen who argues from a
  recap argues from someone else's compression.
- Do not reduce the Constitution to a civics memorization drill. The point
  is the design, not the list.
- Do not sermonize the founding. Read the words. Name the feature and the
  tension. Stop.
- Do not pretend the model's summary is the same as reading the document.
  The compression loses what the compressor chose not to keep.
